How to Price Freelance Work Without Undercharging

A practical framework for turning your time, project costs, payment fees and desired profit into a quote you can confidently send to a client.

Why freelancers undercharge

Many freelancers start with one question: “What will the client accept?” A better starting point is “What price makes this project worthwhile for me?”

Underpricing often happens when you count only the hours spent doing the visible work. A project can also include client calls, research, revisions, administration, software, internet costs, taxes and payment or marketplace fees.

Your quote should account for the real cost of delivering the work—not just the time you spend typing, designing or coding.

Step 1: Estimate the real time required

List every major activity required to complete the project.

  • Discovery and research
  • Planning and communication
  • Production or development
  • Testing and quality checks
  • Revisions
  • Final delivery and administration

Add a reasonable buffer when the scope is uncertain. The goal is not to inflate the quote; it is to avoid pretending that unpredictable work takes zero time.

Step 2: Set a sustainable hourly target

Your target hourly rate should reflect your skill level, business costs and income goal. It does not have to be the same rate for every client or every type of project.

Base labour value = estimated project hours × target hourly rate

For example, if a project needs 15 hours and your target is $20 per hour, the base labour value is $300 before project expenses and transaction fees.

Step 3: Add direct project costs

Include expenses that exist because you are doing this particular project. Examples include paid assets, special software, stock media, domain registration, printing, subcontracting or other approved project expenses.

Project cost before fees = labour value + direct project costs

Step 4: Account for payment and platform fees

A quoted price is not necessarily the amount you receive. A marketplace, payment processor or other service may deduct a percentage, a fixed fee, or both.

If a client pays $500 but fees reduce your receipt to $475, your effective earnings are based on $475—not $500.

When fees are significant, calculate them before sending the quote so you do not accidentally pay the cost out of your intended profit.

Step 5: Decide whether you are pricing by hour or by project

Hourly pricing can be useful when the scope is uncertain or the work is naturally time-based. Project pricing can be better when the deliverables are clear and the client is buying an outcome rather than a number of hours.

Even when you quote a fixed project price, use an internal hourly calculation. It helps you check whether the project makes financial sense.

Step 6: Check your profit margin

After accounting for labour, direct costs and fees, ask what remains for profit and business growth.

Net amount = client price − project costs − fees

A healthy quote should leave room for more than immediate personal income. Your business may need to cover equipment, software, marketing, downtime, learning and future projects.

AfriDesk tip: Do not compete only by being cheaper. Make your proposal clearer, define the deliverables, explain the value and make the payment terms easy to understand.

A simple pricing workflow

  1. Estimate the total hours.
  2. Multiply hours by your target rate.
  3. Add direct project costs.
  4. Account for platform and payment fees.
  5. Review the resulting profit.
  6. Check that the scope and revisions are clearly defined.
  7. Send the client a professional proposal with the final project price.
