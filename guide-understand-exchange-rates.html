How to Understand Exchange Rates When Getting Paid Internationally

The exchange rate can change what you finally receive. Learn how to read it, compare it and account for conversion costs when pricing international work.

Why the exchange rate matters

If a client pays you in one currency and you receive or spend the money in another, the exchange rate affects your final amount. A payment that looks attractive in the client's currency may produce a very different result after conversion.

This is why international payment decisions should consider both fees and the exchange rate used for conversion.

Start with the original payment

Record the amount the client agreed to pay and the currency. Do not begin by looking only at the converted amount in your local currency.

Example: If a client owes you 500 USD, record 500 USD first. Then record the exchange rate, conversion result and any applicable charges separately.

Understand the quoted rate

An exchange rate tells you how much of one currency corresponds to another. The important question is not simply whether a rate looks good; it is how the rate used for your transaction compares with the rate you expected and what amount you actually receive.

When reviewing a conversion, record the rate shown by the relevant payment service or financial institution and keep the transaction details with your payment record.

Look beyond the headline rate

A payment service may present a conversion rate while other costs are applied separately or reflected in the conversion result. Consider the complete transaction rather than assuming the displayed rate is your only cost.

  • Original payment amount
  • Exchange rate used
  • Transaction or platform fee
  • Conversion cost or spread, where known
  • Withdrawal or receiving charge
  • Final amount available to you

Compare the amount you expected with the amount you received

For each payment, keep both the original currency amount and the final amount received. This helps you see whether differences came from transaction fees, conversion or other charges.

Do not confuse a currency conversion with profit

A larger number after conversion does not necessarily mean you earned more. If your costs, time and payment charges are also higher, your actual profit may be lower.

For pricing decisions, combine currency calculations with your fee and project-cost calculations. The Freelancer Pricing Calculator can help you build a project price around your costs, fees and target profit.

Use a simple exchange-rate record

For every international payment, record:

  1. Client payment amount and original currency.
  2. Date of the transaction.
  3. Exchange rate or conversion information shown.
  4. Fees charged.
  5. Amount received in the final currency.
  6. Any withdrawal or receiving charge.

This creates a useful history you can review when deciding which payment methods work best for your business.

When setting your price

If you have a target take-home amount, work backwards rather than simply accepting a converted figure. Allow for payment fees and realistic conversion costs before deciding what to quote.

This is especially useful when a client wants to pay in a currency different from the one you normally use for your business expenses.

Exchange-rate checklist

  • ☐ Record the original payment currency.
  • ☐ Record the amount agreed with the client.
  • ☐ Check the conversion rate that will apply.
  • ☐ Account for transaction and conversion costs.
  • ☐ Estimate your final amount before accepting the payment terms.
  • ☐ Record the actual rate and amount received afterward.
  • ☐ Use your records to improve future pricing decisions.
