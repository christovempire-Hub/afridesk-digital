How to Calculate Your True Freelance Hourly Rate

Your advertised hourly rate is not necessarily what you earn. A true freelance hourly rate accounts for the hours you can actually bill, business costs, payment fees, taxes where applicable, and the time you spend running your business.

Why your hourly rate matters

A freelancer can charge what looks like a good hourly rate and still earn less than expected. The reason is simple: not every hour you work is a billable hour.

You may spend time finding clients, writing proposals, answering messages, revising work, managing invoices, learning new skills, marketing yourself, and handling administration. Those hours still have a cost.

The goal: set a rate that supports your desired income after realistic working hours and business costs—not simply a rate that sounds competitive.

1. Start with your income goal

Decide how much you want your freelance business to generate over a month or year. Use a realistic target rather than a number based only on what you hope to earn.

If your target is monthly income, you can begin with:

Annual income goal = Monthly income goal × 12

Then add the costs that your business needs to cover. Your rate has to generate enough revenue to cover both your personal income target and the costs of operating the business.

2. Estimate your real working hours

Do not assume that every hour in your workday can be sold to a client. Separate your time into billable and non-billable work.

Billable time

  • Designing or developing client work.
  • Writing, editing, research, or analysis for a client.
  • Client meetings that are included in your pricing.
  • Other work directly connected to a paid project.

Non-billable time

  • Finding clients and sending proposals.
  • Marketing and networking.
  • Invoicing and payment follow-up.
  • Business administration.
  • Learning and professional development.
  • Planning, bookkeeping, and other operations.

Estimating billable hours realistically is one of the most important parts of setting your rate.

3. Calculate your annual business costs

List recurring and project-related costs that help you deliver your services.

  • Internet and communication.
  • Software and subscriptions.
  • Equipment and maintenance.
  • Cloud storage or hosting.
  • Professional services.
  • Marketplace or platform fees.
  • Payment processing and withdrawal fees.
  • Other legitimate business expenses.

Some costs are fixed while others increase as your business grows. Keep both categories in your calculation.

4. Use a simple rate formula

A useful starting point is to divide the amount your business needs to generate by your realistic annual billable hours.

Base hourly rate = (Income goal + Annual business costs) ÷ Annual billable hours

This gives you a starting rate before considering a separate profit buffer, taxes, or project-specific costs that should be handled separately.

5. Account for payment and platform fees

If a platform or payment method takes a percentage of your revenue, your advertised rate may need to be higher so that the fee does not reduce your target income.

Also consider fixed transaction fees, currency conversion costs, withdrawal fees, and other charges that apply to the way you get paid.

6. Example calculation

Imagine a freelancer wants to generate the equivalent of ₦3,600,000 in personal income over a year and expects annual business costs of ₦600,000.

They estimate that after non-billable work, leave, holidays, client gaps, and administration, they can realistically bill 1,200 hours during the year.

(₦3,600,000 + ₦600,000) ÷ 1,200 = ₦3,500/hour

So ₦3,500/hour is the approximate base rate before additional adjustments such as platform/payment fees, taxes where applicable, or a desired profit buffer.

7. Add a profit buffer

A sustainable freelance business should not operate with no room for unexpected costs or slow periods. A profit buffer can help you handle equipment replacement, client gaps, price increases, reinvestment, and business growth.

You can build this into your target rate rather than treating every extra amount as personal income.

8. Your minimum rate is not your quote

Your calculated hourly rate is a useful internal benchmark. It does not mean you must tell every client, “My rate is exactly this amount per hour.”

For many projects, project-based pricing can be better. Estimate the hours required, add costs and fees, consider the value and complexity of the work, and then provide a project price.

That is why your internal hourly rate should help you make decisions even when your client sees a fixed project price.

9. Review your rate regularly

Your rate should change as your business changes. Review it when:

  • Your skills and experience improve.
  • Your software or business costs increase.
  • Your demand increases.
  • You consistently have more work than available hours.
  • Your actual billable hours are lower than expected.
  • Payment or platform costs change.

Keep a simple record of your actual hours, revenue, costs, and fees. After several projects, your real data will be more useful than guessing.
