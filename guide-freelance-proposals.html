How to Write a Freelance Proposal That Wins Clients

A good proposal does more than describe your service. It shows the client that you understand the problem, have a clear plan and can deliver the result.

Many freelancers make proposals harder than they need to be. They write long introductions, list every skill they have and focus heavily on themselves. A stronger proposal is client-focused: it connects the client's problem to your solution, sets clear expectations and makes the next step easy.

1. Start with the client's problem

Before talking about your experience, show that you understand what the client needs. Restate the project in practical terms and identify the outcome they are looking for.

For example, instead of saying “I am a web designer with five years of experience,” explain what you will help accomplish: “I will create a fast, mobile-friendly landing page designed to turn visitors into enquiries.”

2. Explain your solution clearly

Describe how you intend to approach the work without overwhelming the client with technical details. Focus on the result and the major stages of the project.

  • What you will create or deliver
  • How the work will be approached
  • What the client can expect at the end

3. Define the deliverables

Vague promises create misunderstandings. List the actual things the client will receive.

  • Website or landing page
  • Number of pages or screens
  • Copywriting or content setup
  • Revisions included
  • Source files or handover materials

The more clearly you define the scope, the easier it becomes to protect yourself from scope creep.

4. Set a realistic timeline

Give the client a simple schedule. You do not need to describe every hour of your work. Instead, identify meaningful milestones such as discovery, first draft, revisions and final delivery.

Example: Discovery and planning → First version → Client feedback → Revisions → Final delivery

5. Present your price confidently

Your price should be connected to the scope. Avoid dropping a number into the proposal without explaining what it covers.

If you are unsure what to charge, first calculate your time, business costs, payment fees and desired profit. Then turn that calculation into a project price.

6. Include terms and next steps

Make the decision process simple. State what happens after the client accepts the proposal, including payment arrangements, project start and communication expectations.

  • What payment is required to begin
  • When work will start
  • How feedback will be provided
  • How additional work will be handled

7. Keep the proposal focused

A proposal does not need to be a full autobiography. Remove anything that does not help the client understand the project or make a decision.

AfriDesk tipThink of your proposal as a bridge between the client's problem and a clear buying decision. The client should finish reading it knowing what you will do, what they will receive, how long it will take and what it costs.

A simple freelance proposal structure

  1. Client and project name
  2. Short project overview
  3. Your understanding of the client's goal
  4. Proposed solution
  5. Deliverables
  6. Timeline
  7. Project price
  8. Terms and next steps

Turn your proposal into a repeatable system

Once you have a strong proposal structure, do not rewrite everything from scratch for every client. Keep a reusable template and customise the parts that matter: the client's problem, your proposed solution, deliverables, timeline and price.

This saves time while keeping each proposal personal and relevant.
